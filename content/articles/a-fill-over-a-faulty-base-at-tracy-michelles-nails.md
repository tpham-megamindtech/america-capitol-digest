*Customer Service Bulletin - Technical Foundations & Accountability Feature*

A set of nails is only as strong as the foundation it's built on, and a fill can't rescue a base that was already compromised. This report on Tracy Michelle's Nails is especially useful because the reviewer is a nail technician herself, and she puts her finger on the exact technical decision that doomed the result. It's a clear lesson in not building on bad work, and in owning the outcome rather than the client.

## Chipped Within a Day, Twice

The set was done by a newer technician, and it failed fast. The client reported a chipped nail and chipped acrylic in less than 24 hours, and had to return twice. Two days in, she said, the nails already looked like they'd been done for a week. Chipping that quickly, more than once, is a clear retention failure that points back to prep, product, or technique, not to normal wear.

## Building on a Base You Already Know Is Faulty

Here is the heart of the case, in the client's own professional read of it. If the technician recognized that her previous overlay wasn't done properly, then putting another fill on top of that faulty base was setting the work up to fail from the start. As she put it, if you weren't sure about the quality of the service you were providing, you shouldn't have touched the nails, and you can't blame the client for bad quality service. A fill assumes a sound foundation. When the base is questionable, the right move is to remove and rebuild it, or to pause and talk it through with the client, never to build over a problem and hope it holds.

## Media Expert's Perspective

The technical principle here is one every salon can train on: never build on a foundation you know is compromised. If a base overlay is faulty, address it directly, either by removing and redoing it or by explaining the situation to the client before proceeding, because a fill placed over bad work simply carries the problem forward and chips within days. Two things support that. First, newer technicians need mentoring and a quick quality check from a senior tech, especially on structural calls like this one. Second, when work fails, the salon owns it. Blaming the client for a result the salon produced only adds insult to a set that's already falling apart. Get the foundation right, or be honest that it isn't, and the chips, the return trips, and the review all disappear.
