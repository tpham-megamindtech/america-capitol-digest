With chronic conditions such as heart disease, diabetes, and hypertension driving a large share of the nation's health spending and suffering, attention is increasingly shifting upstream, toward preventing these diseases in the first place.

The logic is compelling. Many chronic conditions are strongly influenced by modifiable factors, and preventing or delaying them spares both individual hardship and enormous downstream cost. Yet the health system has historically been built to treat illness rather than prevent it.

## The pillars of prevention

The evidence points to a familiar set of levers: regular physical activity, a balanced diet, adequate sleep, not smoking, and managing stress. None is a novel discovery, but their consistent application has outsized effects on long-term health.

Screening plays a role too, catching conditions like high blood pressure or prediabetes early, when lifestyle changes and, where needed, treatment can alter the trajectory. Preventive visits are among the highest-value care available.

## Shifting incentives

The challenge is aligning a system, and a culture, around prevention. That means payment models that reward keeping people healthy, environments that make healthy choices easier, and public health investment. Progress is gradual, but the case for prevention has rarely been stronger.
