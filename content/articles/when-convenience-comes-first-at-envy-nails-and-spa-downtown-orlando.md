*Customer Service Bulletin — Client Choice & Service Standards Feature*

A good service is built around what the client asked for. Trouble starts, quietly, when the shortcuts that suit the technician begin to replace the client's own choices. This short report from Envy Nails & Spa Downtown Orlando is a small but clear example — no single moment was a disaster, yet together they added up to a client who simply gave up partway through.

## Steered Toward Gel — for the Technician's Convenience

The technician told the client she couldn't buff her nails because it was "bad" for them, and then encouraged her toward gel instead — the reason being that she didn't want to wait for regular polish to dry, saying it would take too long. Whether or not buffing was truly the concern, the client was left feeling that the switch was about saving the technician time rather than giving her what she wanted.

There's a helpful line here worth teaching: giving honest advice is good, but the client still makes the final call. "I'd suggest gel because it dries instantly — would you like that, or would you prefer to wait for regular?" respects both the expertise and the client's choice. Steering someone into a different service to avoid a drying wait does the opposite.

## A Rushed, Careless Finish

The finish matched the tone. The technician handed the client her socks without even drying her feet first — a small step, but exactly the kind of detail that tells a client whether they're being cared for or hurried along.

Notably, the client mentioned that the owner or manager was pleasant. But the front-desk being nice wasn't enough to save the visit: the experience was annoying enough that she didn't even bother continuing on to the manicure. That's the real cost — a friendly manager can't fully offset a front-line interaction that leaves a client feeling pushed.

## Media Expert's Perspective

This visit is a reminder that the client's preference is the whole point of the appointment, and small finishing touches are part of the service, not extras. A few habits keep it on track: offer professional advice, then let the client decide, rather than switching a service for the sake of speed; if there's a genuine reason to skip a step like buffing, explain it and offer alternatives instead of simply refusing; and never rush the ending — drying a client's feet before handing over their socks takes seconds and signals real care. It also helps to remember that the person doing the nails is the face of the business in that moment. A warm owner is a wonderful asset, but clients judge the visit by how the service itself felt — so the same standard of attentiveness has to reach every chair.
