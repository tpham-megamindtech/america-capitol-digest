The latest population estimates confirm a migration pattern that has held for most of the decade: Americans are still moving to the Sun Belt. Texas, Florida, and the Carolinas posted the largest gains, while several metropolitan areas in the Northeast and industrial Midwest saw modest declines.

Demographers attribute the shift to a familiar mix of factors: lower housing costs, warmer weather, and the spread of remote and hybrid work that loosened the tie between where people live and where their employers are based.

## Winners and laggards

Fast-growing metros around Austin, Raleigh, and Tampa continued to add residents, straining infrastructure and pushing up rents that were once a selling point. City planners in those regions are grappling with water supply, road capacity, and school construction.

Meanwhile, some older cities are stabilizing after years of losses, buoyed by immigration and by younger residents drawn to lower costs than the coasts. The net national growth rate remains modest, held down by an aging population and lower birth rates.

The estimates carry political weight as well, offering an early hint of how congressional seats and Electoral College votes could shift after the next full count. For now, the trend lines point steadily toward the South and West.
