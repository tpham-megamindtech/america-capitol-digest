*Customer Service Bulletin, Focus & Emergency Hand-Off Feature*

A client's time is part of what they're paying for, and undivided attention is part of the service. This report from Luxury Nail Spa is a useful one because the technician actually had a genuine reason to be distracted, which makes it less a story about a "bad employee" and more about the systems that should catch these moments. When something urgent comes up, the professional answer isn't to power through a distracted service; it's to hand the client to someone who can give them full attention.

## A Quick Polish That Took Over 30 Minutes

The client came in for a very simple request: a quick polish change, the kind of service that usually takes ten to fifteen minutes. Instead, the technician kept her phone in her lap and was on it throughout, which stretched the polish out to more than thirty minutes. "Unbelievable," the client noted, watching a screen take priority while she sat waiting was the part that stuck with her.

## A Real Situation — and the Better Way to Handle It

To her credit, the technician explained what was going on: she had lost her luggage and needed to sort it out with someone. And to her credit, the client was understanding about it, "that's fine," she said. But she also put her finger on the real fix: if the technician was tied up with something urgent, someone else should have done her nails. The issue wasn't that a staff member had an emergency; it was that the salon let a client absorb the cost of it.

This is the reassuring part for staff: everyone has days when something urgent lands mid-shift, and that's human. The professional move in that moment is simply to flag it and hand the client to an available colleague, so the guest still gets a focused, on time service and the staffer gets space to deal with their situation.

## Media Expert's Perspective

The lesson here is undivided attention, backed by a simple hand-off habit. During a service, the phone belongs away from the workstation, not in the lap, even a quick glance tells the client the screen matters more than they do. And when a genuine emergency comes up, the salon should make it easy and expected to say, "I need a few minutes — let me have a colleague take great care of you." That single practice turns a potential one-star visit into a non-event: the client still gets a prompt, attentive service, and the team member is supported rather than stuck delivering a distracted one. A guest will happily forgive an emergency they never had to pay for in wasted time.
