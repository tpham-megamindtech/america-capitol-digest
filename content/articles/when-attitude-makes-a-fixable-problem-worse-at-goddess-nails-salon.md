*Customer Service Bulletin - Professional Conduct & Complaint Handling Feature*

Most of the problems in this review were small and fixable on their own: an uneven shape, a polish that peeled early, a price that needed clarifying. What turned them into a lasting one-star experience was how the salon responded to each of them. This report from Goddess Nails Salon is a useful study in a single idea: conduct, far more than any one technical slip, decides whether a client forgives a visit or warns everyone away from it.

## A Rushed Set and a Hygiene Concern

The client felt the session was rushed, leaving her nails uneven and messy. Just as worrying to her, the tools didn't appear to be sanitized and the space felt unclean overall. Whether or not the tools were in fact sterilized, the impression alone is a problem, because clients judge safety by what they can see. Visible sanitation habits (opening sealed or clearly disinfected tools in front of the client, a tidy station) are what turn that anxiety into confidence.

## When a Small Correction Met Irritation

When the client mentioned the shape wasn't right, the technician grew irritated and, rather than reshaping evenly, simply made the nails shorter. The polish then began chipping the very next day. On the first complaint, to the salon's credit, they did fix it.

The second visit is where conduct unravelled. The technician was openly rude, called the client "picky," and, by the client's account, made false claims that the earlier issue had been about disliking the color and needing a redo. Worst of all, these pointed comments were made in front of other customers. Embarrassing a client publicly is one of the most damaging things a staff member can do, and it is entirely avoidable: any concern about a client is a private, calm conversation, never a performance for the room.

## A Warranty Request Refused

The real issue, the client explained, was never the color. It was that the shellac polish started peeling the day after her appointment, a clear sign of an application problem. This time the salon was unhelpful and refused to fix it. She left with uneven nails and had them corrected at another salon, where, tellingly, the technician was friendly and genuinely concerned that the work hadn't been done right. That contrast is the whole lesson in miniature: the same problem, handled with warmth, produces a happy client instead of a departing one.

## Pricing That Didn't Match the Website

Finally, the bill came in higher than the prices listed on the salon's own website. When the client questioned it, the receptionist answered with a dismissive shrug. Charging above your posted prices is confusing at best, and meeting a fair question with indifference removes any chance to explain or make it right.

## Media Expert's Perspective

The thread running through every part of this visit is attitude, and that is actually encouraging, because attitude is coachable. None of the underlying problems were fatal on their own; a peeling shellac and an uneven shape are ten-minute fixes, and a pricing question is a thirty-second explanation. What lost this client was the response to each: irritation instead of patience, public comments instead of a private word, a refusal instead of a redo, and a shrug instead of an answer. A few standards would have changed everything. Keep sanitation visible so clients feel safe. Treat every correction as normal and welcome, never as a personal slight. Never discuss a client's complaint in front of others. Honor listed prices, and answer questions about them plainly. And when work fails early, fix it willingly, because the friendly salon down the street that ended up redoing these nails is exactly where this client, and her future recommendations, went instead.
