Antibiotic resistance, the gradual erosion of once-reliable drugs as bacteria evolve to evade them, remains a slow-moving but serious public health threat, and experts warn that complacency could prove costly.

The problem is a consequence of the medicines' own success. The more antibiotics are used, and overused, the more selective pressure bacteria face to develop resistance. Over time, some infections have grown harder to treat with standard drugs.

## Stewardship

A central response is antibiotic stewardship: using the drugs only when needed, choosing the right one, and completing courses appropriately. Hospitals have built stewardship programs, and clinicians are increasingly mindful of prescribing, including resisting pressure to prescribe for viral illnesses antibiotics cannot treat.

The other half of the equation is developing new antibiotics, a scientifically hard and commercially unattractive endeavor that has lagged. Experts have long called for incentives to revive the pipeline before existing options weaken further.

## A shared responsibility

Addressing resistance requires action on many fronts, from prescribing practices to agricultural use of antibiotics to global coordination. The threat is not a sudden crisis, experts emphasize, which is precisely why sustained attention now is the best way to prevent a graver one later.
