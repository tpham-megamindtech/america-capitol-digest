The tax-advantaged 529 plan, long the workhorse of college savings, has grown more flexible in recent years, easing a worry that kept some families on the sidelines: what happens if the money is not needed for tuition.

Contributions grow tax-free and can be withdrawn tax-free for qualified education expenses. The plans are run by states, and many offer a state tax break for residents who contribute, though the federal treatment is what draws most savers.

## New flexibility

Rule changes have expanded what counts as a qualified expense and, notably, now allow a limited rollover of unused funds into a retirement account for the beneficiary, subject to conditions. That change addresses the fear of trapped money.

Funds can also cover apprenticeships and a capped amount of student loan repayment, broadening the plans beyond the traditional four-year path. For families weighing varied futures, that versatility matters.

## Starting early

The plans reward time, since tax-free compounding is most powerful over many years. Even modest, automated contributions from a child's early years can grow substantially. Grandparents can contribute too, a strategy that also carries estate-planning benefits worth discussing with an advisor.
