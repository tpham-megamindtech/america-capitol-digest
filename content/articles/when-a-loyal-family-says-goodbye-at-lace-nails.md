*Customer Service Bulletin - Retention, Complaint Handling & Respect Feature*

Losing a loyal, long-standing family of clients is one of the costliest things that can happen to a salon, and it rarely happens over a single bad day. This report on Lace Nails comes from customers who, in their own words, "used to love" the place and have spent thousands of dollars there over time. What pushed them out was not one incident but a pattern: quality that slipped, fixes that were resisted, and a growing feeling that they were being taken advantage of.

## From Regulars to "Not Going Back"

The family described a clear decline. Nails that once lasted now fall off within days. In their most recent visit, a set stayed on for only two days. The quality had dropped so much that they'd been going in every two weeks just to keep up, despite paying $100 or more each time. After years of loyalty and considerable spending, they've decided they will not be going back.

## Pushback and Blame Instead of a Fix

The harder problem was how the salon responded when they asked for help. Requesting a fix, even after paying a premium, was consistently met with pushback and with blame placed on the client's teenage daughters. Over time this created something no salon should ever cause: the family said they were "literally scared to call" and ask for the nails to be fixed. When a paying customer is afraid to raise a problem, that is the clearest possible sign the complaint process has broken down. A redo request should be welcomed as a chance to make things right, never treated as an accusation to push back against, and the fault for a set that fails in two days rests with the application, not with the client.

## A Concern About How Young Clients Are Treated

The family also voiced a serious perception: that the salon "absolutely take advantage of young girls and teenagers." Whether or not it was intended, teenage clients feeling singled out or blamed is something to take very seriously. Younger clients deserve exactly the same respect, patience, and fair treatment as anyone else, and extra care that they never feel pressured or at fault. A salon's reputation with families depends on it.

## Media Expert's Perspective

This is a story about trust eroding, not about one ruined set. The encouraging part is that everything here is fixable, and the family's long loyalty shows the relationship was once strong. Three things would turn it around. First, the core issue: nails failing within days points to an application and prep problem that needs attention, because no amount of good service makes up for work that doesn't last. Second, the complaint culture: make asking for a fix easy, warm, and free, so no one is ever scared to call, and never respond to a failed set by blaming the client. Third, respect for every client regardless of age: young clients should leave feeling looked after, not taken advantage of. A family that once loved a salon is the easiest kind of client to keep and among the hardest to win back, which is exactly why the fear-to-ask moment should have been treated as an emergency long before it became a final straw.
