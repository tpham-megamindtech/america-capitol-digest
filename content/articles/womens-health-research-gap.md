A long-standing gap in medical research, the historic underrepresentation of women in studies and the underinvestment in conditions that primarily affect them, is finally drawing sustained attention, new funding, and a wave of research.

For decades, much clinical research was conducted predominantly on men, on the flawed assumption that findings would translate. The result is a knowledge base thinner on how diseases present, progress, and respond to treatment in women.

## Expanding the science

The renewed focus spans a broad range of areas, from cardiovascular disease, which can present differently in women, to conditions like endometriosis and menopause that have been chronically understudied relative to their prevalence and impact.

Advocates and researchers argue that closing the gap is not only a matter of equity but of better medicine for everyone, since a fuller understanding of biological differences improves care across the board.

## Momentum and caution

The increased investment and awareness mark real progress, though researchers caution that closing gaps built over generations will take time and sustained commitment. The encouraging shift, they say, is that the deficit is now widely recognized as a problem worth solving.
