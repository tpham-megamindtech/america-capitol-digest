*Customer Service Bulletin - Time Communication & Technical Skill Feature*

A late arrival is one of the most ordinary situations a salon deals with, and how it's handled says a lot. Two things matter most: telling the client about any time limit right away, and making sure the effort to catch up doesn't quietly lower the quality of the work. This report on Lace Nails is a useful example of both slipping at once.

## A Time Problem Raised Too Late

The client arrived about ten minutes late and, importantly, wasn't told of any issue at check-in. It was only midway through her pedicure that she was informed the salon might not be able to do her manicure because of the late arrival. In the end they agreed to do her nails, but only after she offered to cut her pedicure short so there would be enough time for her hands.

That's the heart of the first problem: the client ended up sacrificing part of a service she was paying for, to solve a scheduling concern that should have been raised the moment she walked in. If a late arrival genuinely affects what can be done, the kindest and clearest move is to say so at check-in, so the client can decide up front rather than being caught off guard mid-service.

## Rushed Acrylics and the Wrong Tool

The work that followed showed the strain of the rush. The acrylic nails came out uneven, and the filing was so thick the client said it felt like "play-doh." To shorten the nails, the technician used nail clippers to cut the acrylic, which caused them to break and left uneven lengths.

Each of these is a fixable technical habit. Acrylic should be applied in thin, even layers and refined so it feels natural, not bulky. And acrylic should be shortened with a proper tip cutter or an e-file, never standard nail clippers, which crack and split the enhancement rather than cutting it cleanly.

## Media Expert's Perspective

This visit went sideways on two fronts that reinforce each other: communication and technique. The late arrival wasn't the real issue, because a ten-minute delay is routine. The issue was letting the client discover the time problem halfway through, so that fixing it fell on her, and then letting the resulting rush show up in the work. The fixes are simple and cost nothing. Flag any time limit at check-in and offer options clearly (for example, "we can do a shorter pedicure so there's time for your manicure, or rebook the manicure. Which would you prefer?"), so the choice is the client's and made early. Then protect the quality regardless of the clock: thin, even acrylic and the correct cutting tool. Handled that way, the same appointment ends with a client who felt informed and well cared for, instead of one who left frustrated.
