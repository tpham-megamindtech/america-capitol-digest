A deepening shortage of primary care physicians is straining access to routine medical care across the country, and health systems are scrambling to adapt with new models that lean on teams, technology, and expanded roles for other clinicians.

The gap has been widening for years, driven by an aging population that needs more care, a wave of physician retirements, and a training pipeline that steers many new doctors toward higher-paying specialties.

## New models of care

In response, health systems are increasingly relying on nurse practitioners and physician assistants, team-based practices, and expanded use of telehealth to stretch scarce physician time. The goal is to preserve access even where doctors are thin on the ground.

The consequences of the shortage are real: longer waits for appointments, rushed visits, and gaps in the preventive care that keeps small problems from becoming big ones. Rural and underserved communities feel the strain most acutely.

## Longer-term fixes

Experts say durable solutions require investment in the primary care pipeline, from training slots to loan forgiveness to payment reforms that value the relationship-based work primary care depends on. Without such changes, they warn, the adaptations now underway will only partly offset a growing need.
