Wearable devices are steadily edging from simple fitness trackers toward tools with genuine health utility, gaining features that can detect irregular heart rhythms, track sleep patterns, and flag changes worth a doctor's attention.

The trajectory is striking. Sensors that once merely counted steps now capture a growing array of physiological signals, and some features have earned regulatory clearance for specific medical uses, blurring the line between consumer gadget and health device.

## Promise and pitfalls

The potential is real. A wearable that alerts someone to an irregular heart rhythm can prompt a timely evaluation, and continuous data can help people and clinicians spot trends. For motivated users, the feedback can support healthier habits.

The pitfalls are equally real. Consumer devices can generate false alarms, misinterpreted readings, and anxiety over data that lacks clinical context. Experts caution that a wearable is not a diagnostic tool and that its outputs need careful, professional interpretation.

## A measured view

The consensus is one of measured optimism. Used thoughtfully, as a supplement to medical care rather than a substitute, wearables can contribute to health. The key, clinicians say, is understanding both what these devices can do and, just as importantly, what they cannot.
