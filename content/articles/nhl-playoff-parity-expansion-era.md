Parity in the NHL has reached new heights, producing a league where, on a given night, any team can beat any other. The competitive balance has made for unpredictable seasons and a postseason where upsets are routine rather than shocking.

The structure of the league rewards depth and sound management over the accumulation of stars, since a salary system limits how much talent any one team can stockpile. The result is a compressed field where the gap between best and worst is narrow.

## Depth over stars

The teams that succeed tend to roll four capable lines, get contributions throughout the roster, and receive steady goaltending, the position that can single-handedly swing a playoff series. Building that kind of depth requires patience and shrewd drafting.

Sustaining success is especially hard. Dynasties, once a feature of the sport, have become rare, as the same structure that enables underdogs also makes it difficult to keep a championship core intact over many years.

## Playoff chaos

The postseason distills the parity into its purest form, with tightly matched teams and the thin margins of overtime hockey. For fans, the unpredictability is a feature, not a flaw: in a league this balanced, hope springs eternal for nearly everyone.
